01

Taking Sheldon — the checkpoints

Kazakhstan requires no rabies titre test for dogs of any origin, no import permit for one or two pets travelling with you, and no quarantine when compliant. The most relaxed of your feasible options. The only gate is a valid rabies shot.

Step 1 · India

ISO microchip

15-digit ISO 11784/11785 chip, implanted before the rabies vaccination; number matches every document.

Step 2 · India

Rabies vaccination — valid before travel

Kazakhstan asks for at least 21 days old and no more than 12 months old at entry — but Turkish Airlines requires 30 days, and the airline is the one that decides whether he boards. Work to the stricter rule: shot done by about 23 October 2026.

the one date that governs everything
Step 3 · India

Vet health certificate (within 5 days)

International/EAEU-form certificate, government-vet signed — healthy + rabies-vaccinated, dated within 5 days of travel. In India this is folded into the AQCS export exam.

Step 4 · India

AQCS Bangalore export certificate + NOC

Book AQCS ~1 week ahead; present Sheldon ~7 days before departure. Export certificate valid 10 days. Keep the originals for re-import.

Step 5 · Almaty

Arrival in Kazakhstan

Enter via Almaty international airport; a vet officer may check chip + papers. No titre, no quarantine, no import permit for 1–2 pets.

Step 6 · Return

Bringing Sheldon home to India

Apply for an Advance NOC from AQCS ≥7 working days before flying back, under the Re-import category (Customs Circular 25/2013), using your original export docs — avoids the 15-day quarantine on fresh imports. Carry a Kazakh vet health certificate dated close to departure.

You asked because of the war in the Middle East. For Kazakhstan the answer is reassuring: it is a long way from that conflict, involved in none of it, and the steadier of your two options. Its risk sits elsewhere — a very long Russian border, and one bad month back in 2022.

What this means in practice

  • The Middle East war: Kazakhstan is thousands of kilometres away and party to none of it. As with Georgia, the only way it touches you is aviation — which is precisely why section 03 routes you through Istanbul instead of the Gulf.
  • The 2022 caveat: January 2022 saw serious unrest and a brief state of emergency, including in Almaty. It has not recurred in the years since, and the country has been politically quiet — but it's the honest reason this scores 7 and not 9 on domestic stability.
  • Russia: Kazakhstan shares one of the world's longest land borders with it. The spillover from the Ukraine war has been economic and diplomatic, not military; there is no fighting and no threat to travellers. Nothing in your itinerary goes near that border — Almaty sits in the far south-east, by China and Kyrgyzstan.
  • Everyday risk: petty crime and pickpocketing in central Almaty, and unlicensed taxi scams — use Yandex Go rather than hailing (which section 12 already recommends). Rural road safety is the genuinely elevated risk, which is the other argument for hiring drivers.
  • Official read: the US State Department rates Kazakhstan Level 1 — exercise normal precautions, its safest tier.
How to read this score: it's our composite judgement across the five factors above — weighted for two tourists and a small dog on a two-week trip, not for a business investing there. It isn't an official index. Re-check the picture about a month before you fly.

Driving & car rental

Be honest with yourselves here: Almaty in late November is not an easy self-drive for first-timers. The city is navigable, but winter brings snow and challenging roads, signage/police lean Russian/Kazakh, and the best nature sits on rough mountain and steppe roads that often need a 4WD and local know-how. Hiring a driver or booking guided day trips is the smart, low-stress default — often barely pricier than renting once you add winter risk.

The honest verdict

  • Around the city: self-drive is fine, but the Yandex Go app (cheap, reliable taxis) makes a rental unnecessary for city days.
  • Big Almaty Lake & the eastern lakes: hire a driver with a 4WD — the winter road to Kaindy from Saty needs it, and a guide handles the border checkpoint above BAL.
  • Paperwork if you rent: physical licence + IDP + a second photo ID + credit card for the deposit; book ahead as the fleet is small.
  • Winter caveat: snow, limited English on the road, and cash-only toll stretches — patience and a local driver save the holiday.

Azerbaijan — the seven days

Base · 6 nights

Icherisheher, the walled city

A UNESCO-listed medieval core still inside its walls: the 12th-century Maiden Tower, the Shirvanshahs' Palace, caravanserais now serving tea, and lanes narrow enough to lose an afternoon in. Step outside the gate and you're on the Boulevard, a long Caspian seafront promenade — flat, breezy and made for walking a dog.

Day trip · 1 hr south

Gobustan rock art & mud volcanoes

Thousands of petroglyphs carved into the rocks over 40,000 years — dancers, boats, aurochs — on a UNESCO-listed hillside above the sea. Then a rutted track to a plateau of mud volcanoes quietly burping cold grey mud. Azerbaijan has more of them than any country on earth. Genuinely strange, and utterly memorable.

Half day · the Absheron

Fire — Ateshgah & Yanar Dag

This is the "land of fire" literally. Ateshgah is a pentagonal fire temple where Zoroastrian and Hindu ascetics tended flames fed by natural gas seeps. Yanar Dag is a hillside that has simply been burning, continuously, for centuries. Best at dusk, and it photographs like nowhere else.

Afternoon · modern Baku

Zaha Hadid's white wave

The Heydar Aliyev Center is one of the most photographed buildings of the century — a single poured curve with no straight lines in it, in a park you can walk around freely. Pair it with the Carpet Museum, built in the shape of a rolled rug, and the Flame Towers lit up after dark.

If you want a village too: Sheki, five hours inland, has the extraordinary 18th-century Khan's Palace — stained-glass shebeke lattices assembled without nails or glue — plus caravanserais and mountain air. It only works as an overnight, not a day trip. Swap it in for the slow Baku day if you'd rather have it.

Must-try — the Azerbaijani table

Persian-leaning and herb-heavy, with more vegetarian room than Georgian food once you get into the qutab and dolma. The Georgian dishes are on the Georgia tab.

Plov

The national centrepiece — saffron rice under a crisp qazmaq crust, with dozens of regional versions.

Qutab

Thin folded flatbreads off a griddle — the herb-and-cheese one is the vegetarian hero here.

Dolma

Peppers, tomatoes and vine leaves stuffed and slow-cooked, served with garlicky yoghurt.

Dushbara

Tiny lamb dumplings in broth, folded small enough to fit several on a spoon. The cold-weather cure.

Tea in an armudu glass

Pear-shaped glasses, drunk through a spoonful of jam rather than sugar. A whole afternoon's institution.

Lunch in a caravanserai

Two old silk-road inns inside the walls now serve lunch in their stone courtyards — touristy, and still worth one long meal.

Getting around

Self-drive in Georgia, don't bother in Azerbaijan. Baku traffic is aggressive, parking inside the old city is effectively impossible, and taxis are cheap enough to make the question moot.

The honest verdict

  • Georgia, days 1–8: a compact self-drive works well for Tbilisi, Mtskheta and Kakheti, with Sheldon riding along. Take a private driver for the Kazbegi day — the Military Road in snow isn't the place to experiment.
  • Baku, days 9–15: no rental. Use Bolt or Uber in the city; the metro is clean and cheap but not for dogs. Icherisheher and the Boulevard are entirely walkable, which is most of your week.
  • Gobustan and the Absheron: hire a car and driver for the day, roughly $60–100. The mud volcano track needs a high-clearance vehicle and local drivers negotiate it routinely; ordinary taxis will refuse.
  • Airport: Heydar Aliyev is about 25 km out — around 30 minutes, and worth pre-booking on the way home with the carrier and paperwork in tow.
